[storytelling, warm] Picture a smoke detector. It's there to protect you. When it senses danger, it goes off loudly, and that's exactly what we want it to do.
Your brain has an alarm system that works in much the same way. It's very old. We share it with many animals, and it helped our ancestors escape predators.
At the heart of this alarm is a small, almond-shaped area called the amygdala, which picks up anything that looks like a threat. It acts very fast, often before you've had time to think. That's why you can jump before you even understand what's happening.
Think of slamming on the brakes to avoid a cyclist. In a split second, before you've even thought about it, your body has already reacted.
[pause 1.5s]
The trouble is that today, this detector often goes off over a slightly burnt piece of toast. A full inbox, a traffic jam, a comment from a coworker. No real danger to your life, but the alarm sounds anyway.
Your body doesn't always tell the difference between a lion and a presentation in front of your boss. In both cases, it gets roughly the same resources ready.
It's a bit as if your phone sounded the same siren for a text from a friend and for a fire alarm.
[pause 2s]
[clear] Here's what happens when the alarm sounds. Two things, as if someone pressed the accelerator.
First, within seconds, your body releases adrenaline. That's what makes your heart beat faster, speeds up your breathing, tenses your muscles, and sometimes gives you sweaty palms or a knot in your stomach.
Meanwhile, anything that isn't urgent takes a back seat. Digestion slows down, for example. Your body puts its resources into action.
Then, a little more slowly, over several minutes, a second pathway takes over. It's a chain linking the brain to small glands sitting on top of the kidneys, and it releases cortisol.
Cortisol mobilizes energy, in particular by raising the sugar available in your blood. It helps your body keep going.
Picture a runner on the starting line. Heart pounding, muscles ready, energy available. That's what adrenaline and cortisol are preparing you for.
All of this is useful, in the short term. It's what lets you react, focus, and push yourself.
[pause 2s]
[warm, clear] But an accelerator isn't enough. Your body also has a brake, the recovery system. It slows the heart, relaxes the muscles, gets digestion going again. It tells your body, it's over, you can rest. Scientists call it the parasympathetic nervous system, but you can simply call it the brake.
Normally, after the alarm, the brake takes over. The problem is when the accelerator stays pressed down, day after day. Studies link this kind of prolonged stress with fatigue, poorer sleep, and other effects on health.
The good news is that you can partly press this brake yourself. And one of the most accessible ways is your breath, especially the out-breath.

[warm, clear] Here's something to explore next. Everyone has their own warning signals. For some, it's a clenched jaw. For others, a knotted stomach, a held breath, or shoulders creeping up.
