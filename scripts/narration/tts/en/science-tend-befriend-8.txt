[clear, storytelling] When we talk about stress, we usually think of two reactions. Fight or flight. A racing heart, muscles ready to spring.
In the year two thousand, an American researcher, Shelley Taylor, and her team suggested adding to that picture.
The team noticed that, under stress, many humans also do something else. They move closer to others. They take care of the most vulnerable, and they seek support.
The researchers called this tend and befriend. Caring for others, and reaching out.
[pause 1.5s]
[warm] Think about what happens after bad news. Often, the first reflex is to call someone, to check on the people we love, to hold our children close. That's exactly it.
Or think of those stormy nights when neighbors who barely say hello suddenly find themselves in the hallway, helping each other out.
[calm, clear] Several hormones seem to be involved in this urge to connect, and researchers are still exploring their exact role.
Shelley Taylor's work focused mainly on women. But other teams have observed that, under stress, men also became more cooperative and generous. This tendency seems to exist in all of us.
[pause 1.5s]
And it's more than a nice idea. Connection with others is one of the strongest buffers against stress.
You can think of it as a cushion between you and the hard knocks of life.
In one study, for example, people had to speak in front of a panel. Those who had spent time with a friend just beforehand showed a smaller stress response in their bodies.
Large health studies also show that people with good social ties are, on average, in better health. What matters isn't necessarily how many friends you have, but the feeling that you can count on someone. Studies even suggest that brief, friendly exchanges, with a neighbor or the person at the bakery, can lift our mood.
[pause 1.5s]
[warm] So when stress rises and you feel like calling someone, that's not weakness. It's your body reaching for a very old, and often very effective, resource.
And connection works both ways. For many people, offering support seems to do good to the giver as well.
