[clear, unhurried] Clinicians, including the American psychiatrist Dan Siegel, have offered an image called the window of tolerance.
Picture a band, a zone, between two extremes. Inside this zone, you can feel emotions, even strong ones, and still be able to think. You can listen, choose, respond rather than react.
It isn't a state of perfect calm. Inside your window, there can be anger, worry, emotion, excitement. But you stay at the controls.
Think of a river flowing between two banks. The water can be calm or choppy, fast or slow. As long as it stays within its banks, it keeps on flowing.
[pause 2s]
[calm, clear] Above the window is over-activation. Your heart pounds, your thoughts race. It's agitation, panic around the corner, anger taking over. Thinking clearly gets hard, and you react on a hair trigger.
Below it is under-activation. Here, it's more like emptiness. A sense of numbness, of being cut off from your feelings, flat. As if someone had turned the dimmer down.
You might want to curl up under the covers, or scroll through your phone without really seeing anything.
Or stare at the same page for ten minutes without taking in a single word.
[gentle] Both states are protective reactions of your nervous system. They're not flaws, and they're not failures.
[pause 2s]
[clear] Here's an example. You get an unpleasant email. Inside your window, you read it, feel the irritation rise, take a breath, and choose how to reply.
Above it, you fire back within a minute, hammering the keyboard, and regret it a little later. Below it, you shut the laptop and can't get anything done for the rest of the evening.
No one stays inside their window all the time. Leaving it is part of life. What matters is knowing how to come back.
[pause 1.5s]
The width of the window varies. Poor sleep, hunger, built-up stress can narrow it. Rest, connection with others, and practice can help widen it.
It's worth saying that this is a model, a map to help you find your bearings, not a precise measurement. But many people find it helps them understand themselves better.
Above all, it points the way. You don't come back to your window the same way when you're too high as when you're too low. Too high, the aim is to come down. Too low, the aim is to come gently back up.
It's like a thermostat. When it's too hot, you don't turn up the heating. When it's too cold, you don't open the window.
