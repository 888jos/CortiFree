[clear, storytelling] You may have heard of the vagus nerve. It's become very fashionable in recent years, so let's untangle what's true from what's fuzzy.
The vagus nerve is one of the longest nerves in your body. It starts at the base of the brain, runs down through the neck, and branches out to the heart, the lungs, and a large part of the digestive system.
Its name comes from the Latin word vagus, which means wanderer. It wanders through your whole torso, visiting many of the places where you feel stress.
It's a major communication line, and it runs both ways. It carries messages from the brain to the organs. But it carries even more from the organs back to the brain. How your stomach is doing, how fast your heart is beating, how your lungs are breathing.
[pause 1.5s]
It's part of the recovery system, the one that slows the heart and supports digestion and rest. You could call it your body's brake, working against the accelerator of stress.
This brake is working all the time, even right now. You don't have to switch it on. You can simply give it a little more room.
It's a bit like your foot hovering near the brake pedal when you drive. It doesn't slam on the brakes. It keeps adjusting your speed.
Researchers track its activity through a measure called heart rate variability, the small, natural variations in time between heartbeats. A healthy heart isn't a metronome. It's constantly speeding up and slowing down, along with the breath.
[pause 2s]

[clear] So what seems to engage this brake? First, slow exhaling. When you breathe out, your heart slows slightly, and the vagus nerve plays a big part in that. Lengthening your out-breath makes use of a natural mechanism.
You can feel it for yourself some time. Place two fingers on your wrist or your neck, and breathe slowly. For many people, the pulse speeds up a little on the in-breath, and slows on the out-breath.
Next, the voice. The vagus nerve also helps control the muscles of the throat and larynx, the ones that let you speak and sing.
And finally, a sense of safety. A familiar face, a gentle voice, a reassuring presence. All of these seem to help the body step out of alert mode.

A few studies, still small, also suggest that bee breath, a closed-mouth humming practice from yoga, may encourage relaxation. And many people simply enjoy the vibration it creates.
