# sleep-story-provence-20 · Summer night in Provence (EN)
[pause 1.5s]
[warm, soft, unhurried] Before we begin, get yourself nice and settled. Find the position where your body feels most at ease, and let the bed hold you.
Tonight, we're heading south. To an old stone house in the hills, on a summer evening.
There's nothing to remember in this story. No plot, no surprises. Just scents, sounds, and the heat of the day slowly fading.
If you fall asleep before the end, that's perfect. Let my voice carry on without you, like a radio left on very low in the next room.
[pause 4s]

[storytelling, calm] It's the end of a long July day, in Provence.
You arrived here a few days ago, at the farmhouse of a friend who has lent it to you for the summer. A big, low house of pale golden stone, with lavender coloured shutters, and a roof of curved tiles, a little crooked, that have taken on every shade of orange and pink.
All afternoon, it's been very hot. A white, motionless heat that made the air shimmer above the fields. You stayed indoors, in the cool of the thick walls, the shutters half closed.
You read a little. You dozed. You felt the cool tiles under your bare feet.
And now, at last, the heat is beginning to ease.
[pause 2s]
You open the kitchen door, the one that leads out to the garden. The air outside is still warm, but it has changed.
It's softer, lighter. A small breeze is coming down from the hills, and it smells of thyme.
The light has changed too. It's no longer white. It's golden and low, stretching the shadows across the dry earth.
The shadow of the fig tree reaches all the way to the wall. The shadow of the well grows long and thin, like the needle of a sundial.
[pause 2s]
You step outside, barefoot. The flagstones in front of the house are still warm.
Their warmth rises into the soles of your feet, a round, gentle heat, stored up all day long. Then you step onto the dry grass, which crackles softly underfoot, and it's cooler.
You pause for a moment by the old well. You rest your hand on its stone rim, worn smooth by the years, warm as a cheek.
Far below, the dark water holds a small circle of sky. A smell of wet stone and moss drifts up towards you.
You let the bucket down on its rope, and it comes up heavy and dripping. You dip your hands in. The water is cool and clear, and you press your wet palms gently to your cheeks, to your neck.
[pause 2s]

The garden slopes gently down, in small terraces held up by dry stone walls. You take the little gravel path that winds between them.
First, there's the lavender.
Rows and rows of lavender, perfectly round, like purple cushions lined up along the path. The flowers are in full bloom. A few late bees are still humming among them, moving from one stem to the next, heavy and slow, before heading home to the hive.
You pass your hand over the stems, barely brushing the flowers. At once, the scent rises.
A strong, clean, slightly peppery scent that stays on your fingers. You bring your hand to your face, and breathe in.
It's the smell of a grandmother's wardrobe, of sheets folded away with a little cloth sachet. The smell of childhood summers.
[pause 2s]
A little further on, against the wall, grows a big rosemary bush. Its small, needle like leaves are dark green, silvery underneath. You pick a sprig.
You roll it between your fingers. Its scent is more resinous, warmer, like a pine forest in the sun.
There's thyme too, in little grey tufts close to the ground. And sage, with leaves as soft and velvety as a rabbit's ear.
All these scents blend in the evening air. They rise from the earth, still warm, and float around you like an invisible scarf.
[pause 2s]

At the end of the path, on the last terrace, stands the old fig tree.
It's an enormous tree, twisted, with a trunk as grey and smooth as an elephant's skin. Its branches spread very low, almost to the ground, and its big rough leaves cast a thick, green, cool shade.
You step under the branches. Suddenly it's cooler. The air smells of milk and honey, that unmistakable scent of fig leaves after a whole day warming in the sun.
The figs are ripe. They hang beneath the leaves, purple, slightly split, swollen with sugar. You pick one.
It's warm and soft in your hand. You open it with your thumbs. Inside, the flesh is pink, almost red, full of tiny seeds.
You eat it slowly, standing under the tree. It's as sweet as jam.
[pause 2s]

All around you, the cicadas are singing.
They've been singing since morning. All day long their song has filled the air, shrill and unbroken, so constant you stopped hearing it. It was part of the heat, like the light.
But now that the sun is sinking, their song is changing.
It becomes less regular. There are pauses. One cicada stops, then another.
Then they start again, a little softer. Like an orchestra playing its final bars, in no hurry.
You stay there under the fig tree, listening.
The song is running out of breath. It comes in waves now. One wave rising from the big pine by the gate.
Another answering from the other side, among the olive trees. Then a silence. Then another wave, fainter.
And little by little, one by one, the cicadas fall silent.
Only one is left, somewhere in an olive tree. It sings on a little longer, alone, as if it wanted the last word. Then it stops too.
[pause 2s]
And the silence arrives.
Not an empty silence. A silence full of small sounds. The rustle of fig leaves in the breeze.
A bird calling two notes in the distance. The tinkle of a goat's bell, on the far side of the hill.
[pause 2s]

From the bottom of the garden, you can see the whole valley. Wheat fields already harvested, the colour of straw.
Rows of olive trees with silver leaves. Vineyards in neat lines, following the curve of the hills.
And on the hill opposite, a little hilltop village, huddled around its church. Its houses are the same colour as the stone of the hill, so it looks as if they simply grew there.
In the bell tower, a bell begins to ring. Seven strokes, slow and spaced out.
The sound crosses the valley and reaches you, softened by distance. Then the last stroke fades, and the air closes over it.
You walk back up towards the house, unhurried, along the gravel path. You pass the lavender again, and the rosemary. You let your fingers trail over the flowers.
[pause 4s]

In front of the house, there's the arbour.
Four old wooden posts, and over them, a trellis covered with vines. The leaves make a living green roof, pierced with little holes of light.
Bunches of grapes hang among the leaves, still green and hard. They'll be ripe at the end of summer.
Under the arbour stands a long wooden table, grey and weathered by the years, with a bench on either side.
That's where you'll have dinner tonight. A very simple dinner.
[pause 2s]
You go back into the kitchen. It's cool and a little dim, with red terracotta tiles on the floor and beams across the ceiling. Bunches of herbs hang upside down to dry, near the window.
You take a big earthenware dish, glazed yellow. You put in tomatoes from the garden, still warm from the sun, and cut them into wedges.
They're red, fleshy, a little uneven. Their juice runs across the board.
You add a drizzle of golden green olive oil. A few grains of coarse salt that crunch between your fingers. Basil leaves, torn by hand, whose scent fills the kitchen at once.
You also take a piece of goat's cheese, a small round loaf, and a handful of black olives, wrinkled and glossy.
You carry it all outside, to the table under the arbour. You go back for a jug of cool water with a slice of lemon in it. And a glass.
[pause 2s]
You sit down on the bench. The wood is smooth, polished by years of summer meals.
You eat slowly. There's no one to wait for, nowhere to go. Just this meal, and the fading light.
The bread crackles between your teeth. The tomato is sweet and sharp at once.
The olive oil tastes faintly of cut grass. The cheese is fresh, a little lemony.
Now and then you sip some water. It's cold, and you feel its coolness travel all the way down.
A faint smell of woodsmoke drifts in from far away, carried on the evening air. Somewhere down in the valley, at another farmhouse, someone is making their dinner too.
[pause 2s]
The neighbour's cat arrives without a sound, along the top of the wall. A tabby, lean and calm. It jumps onto the bench opposite, sits, and watches you eat, blinking slowly.
You give it a little piece of cheese. It takes it delicately, eats it, then curls up on the warm bench and closes its eyes. It will stay there a while, purring, before slipping away into the night.
[pause 2s]
Above you, through the vine leaves, the sky shifts from pale blue to pink, then to mauve.
A swallow darts past, skimming the arbour, then another. They wheel through the evening sky with little high cries, catching the last insects.
Then, little by little, they too disappear.
The sun has set behind the hill. There's only an orange glow above the ridge, where the cypresses and the pines stand out, black.
[pause 2s]
To finish, you take a peach from the basket. A vineyard peach, with downy, dark red skin. You cut it into slices with a small knife.
The juice runs over your fingers, sweet and fragrant. You eat it piece by piece, taking your time.
When you've finished, you stay sitting, your hands resting on the table. The wood is still warm.
You do nothing. You think of nothing in particular. You watch the garden slowly darken.
The colours go out one by one. The purple of the lavender turns grey.
The green of the fig tree turns black. The golden stones of the house turn blue, then grey.
The silence is deep now. A countryside silence. All you can hear, very far away, is a dog barking twice, then falling quiet.
[pause 3s]

[storytelling, slower] You clear the table, unhurried. You carry the dish, the glass and the jug back to the kitchen. You rinse everything under cool water and set it on the draining rack.
Then you go back outside.
At the end of the terrace, near the low wall, there's an old deckchair in striped canvas, blue and white. You pull it a little further from the house, to where the sky is biggest.
You lie down.
Beneath the deckchair, the flagstones are gently giving back the warmth they've held all day. It rises to you through the canvas, like the warmth of a sleeping animal.
The canvas is taut under your back, supple and firm at once. It takes your shape. You rest your arms on the wooden armrests.
You stretch out your legs. You let your head fall back.
The air is mild now. Neither warm nor cool. Exactly the temperature of your skin, so that you can no longer quite tell where your body ends and the night begins.
[pause 3s]
The sky above you is a very deep blue. An ink blue.
And in that blue, a first star appears.
It's just above the hill, all alone, bright and clear. Perhaps it's a planet.
It doesn't twinkle. It shines, calmly.
You watch it. And while you're watching, a second star appears, a little higher. Then a third.
It's as if someone were lighting them one by one, very slowly, taking their time.
You don't see them arrive. You blink, and there they are.
Five. Ten. Twenty.
Here, far from any town, there's no light to hide them. Little by little, the sky fills up, until it's covered.
[pause 2s]
And then you see it. The Milky Way.
A long, pale, milky scarf crossing the sky from one side to the other. Like a cloud of glowing dust, resting there, motionless, forever.
You lie there, watching.
You recognise the Plough, its seven stars in the shape of a saucepan, the handle pointing towards the hill. You follow the edge of the pan with your eyes, and you find the North Star, alone, faithful, never moving.
Around it, slowly, through the night, the whole sky turns. But you can't see it move.
You'd have to stay there for hours. And you have no wish to count the hours.
[pause 3s]
On the hill opposite, the lights of the village go out, one by one. One window, then another.
People are going to bed. Soon only the little street lamp in the square is left, and the outline of the bell tower against the stars.
The crickets have taken over from the cicadas. Their song is softer, steadier.
A small chirr rising from the grass, from everywhere at once, calm and continuous. As if the earth itself were breathing.
Now and then a shooting star crosses the sky. A very fine, very quick streak of light, gone before you can point at it.
You don't need to make a wish. You just watch it pass.
[pause 3s]
The evening breeze still carries the scents of the garden. Lavender, thyme, rosemary. And a new scent, the smell of the earth cooling, finally breathing out the heat of the day.
At the foot of the deckchair, there's an old wool blanket, folded in four. You unfold it over your legs.
It's soft and a little thick, and it smells of the linen cupboard and of lavender. Its fringes tickle your ankles.
[soft, slower] Your breathing is slow. Your arms are heavy on the armrests. Your eyelids are growing heavy too.
The breeze touches your bare arms, your ankles, your face, lightly, like a hand smoothing a sheet. There's nowhere you need to be.
Nothing you need to remember. Only the sky, and the crickets, and the slow, warm night.
[pause 3s]
You could stay there all night. But the air is gently cooling. It's time to go in.
You get up slowly. You fold the deckchair and lean it against the wall. You take one last look at the starry sky.
[pause 4s]

[soft, slow, storytelling] You go into the house. Inside, the thick walls have kept some of the day's coolness.
It's pleasant. The tiles are cool under your feet.
You climb the stone staircase to the bedrooms. The steps are slightly worn in the middle. The wrought iron banister is cool under your hand.
On the landing, a little gecko clings to the wall beside the window, pale and perfectly still, waiting for moths. It doesn't move as you pass. It's part of the house, like the beams and the tiles.
In the little bathroom upstairs, you let cool water run into your cupped hands, and splash it over your face, your neck, your arms. The coolness washes away the dust and the heat of the day. You dry yourself with a cotton towel, a little rough, that smells of Marseille soap.
[pause 2s]
Your room is simple. Whitewashed walls. A chest of drawers in pale wood.
A big iron bed with white sheets. And a window, with its lavender coloured shutters.
You open the window. You push the shutters out, then draw them back, not all the way.
You leave them half open, just enough to let the night air in. Just enough to see a small patch of starry sky.
The song of the crickets comes into the room. And the scent of lavender too, carried on the breeze.
[pause 4s]

[soothing, slower] You slip into bed.
The sheets are cool. Linen sheets, a little coarse, dried in the sun, still smelling of fresh air. Their coolness on your skin, after the heat of the day, is delicious.
You stretch, long and slow. You feel your legs slide through the cool sheets, all the way to the bottom of the bed.
Then you settle. Your head in the pillow. Your arms by your sides.
[pause 3s]
Through the half open shutters, a small rectangle of sky. Three stars, perhaps four.
Very far off, if you listen closely, you can hear the village fountain. A thin trickle of water running, night and day, into its stone basin.
The crickets sing outside. Chirr. Chirr.
Slowly. Steadily.
[pause 3s]
The breeze moves the shutter, barely. It creaks very softly, then stops.
The lavender... the thyme... the earth cooling...
[pause 3s]

The rosemary against the wall. The old fig tree, its figs ripening slowly through the night.
The table under the arbour, empty, beneath the vine leaves.
The deckchair, folded against the wall.
The well, and its shadow that's no longer there.
[pause 3s]
The bees are asleep in their hive, pressed close together, warm, in the scent of wax and honey.
The swallows are asleep under the eaves, in their nests of mud.
The village on the hill opposite is asleep too. Its bell will ring midnight later on, but no one will count the strokes.
[pause 3s]

In the kitchen, the bunches of herbs are still drying, upside down, in the dark.
The terracotta tiles keep their coolness. Somewhere, an old clock makes its small, steady sound.
The cicadas are asleep, clinging to the branches of the pines. They'll sing again tomorrow, when the sun comes back. But not now.
