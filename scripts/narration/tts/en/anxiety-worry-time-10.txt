# anxiety-worry-time-10 · Worry appointment (EN)
[pause 1.5s]
[warm, calm] Welcome to your worry appointment. The name might sound strange. Why make an appointment with the things that worry us?
[pause 1.5s]
Because worries have a habit of showing up whenever they like. In a meeting, in the shower, in the middle of a movie, at three in the morning. And every time, we get swept along.
[clear] The idea behind this technique, widely used in cognitive behavioral therapy, is simple. Instead of worrying all day long, you worry on purpose, for a limited time. And the rest of the time, you're allowed to tell your worries, not now, I'll see you at the next appointment.
Many people find that with a little practice, worries take up less room in their day. Ideally, you'd do this appointment every day at the same time, preferably in the late afternoon, and not right before bed.
Why not right before bed? Because your mind needs a little time to settle afterward. Your bed should stay a place for rest, not for sorting.
[pause 2s]
[calm] If you'd like, grab a sheet of paper and a pen, or open a note on your phone. Writing helps get worries out of your head. But you can also do all of this in your mind.
Sit in a way that keeps you alert but comfortable. Keep your eyes open if you're writing, or close them if you prefer.
[pause 2s]
[soft] Take a calm breath...
[pause 4s]
[grounded] Plant your feet. Feel your back. The appointment begins.
[pause 3s]

[warm, clear] For the next few minutes, you have permission to worry. Really. No need to talk yourself out of it, or to tell yourself it's no big deal.
Let all the worries that are spinning right now come up. The big ones, the small ones, the recurring ones, the new ones, the silly ones too.
Work. A project, a deadline, a tense relationship with a coworker. Money. A bill, an overdraft, an upcoming expense. Health, yours or a loved one's. Family, your relationship, your friends. Your home. The future.
[pause 2s]
Write them down, or name them silently, one by one. One line, a few words per worry. No details, no solutions for now. Just a list.
If a worry seems too embarrassing or too small to note, note it anyway. Every worry is welcome here. The more honest the list, the more useful the sorting will be.
[gentle] If it starts to feel like too much, you can slow down, set your pen down, and feel your feet on the floor for a few seconds before carrying on.
[pause 4s]
[calm] Keep going. Are there any others, hiding behind the first ones?
[pause 2.5s]
Things you avoid looking at? If so, add them too.
[pause 3s]
Notice how your body reacts while you make this list. Maybe some tightening, a quickening. That's normal, and it will settle. Let your out breath lengthen a little.
[pause 3s]
Now look at your list as a whole. Sometimes just seeing your worries written down, in black and white, makes them smaller, or clearer. What was going round and round in your head fits, in the end, into a few lines.
[pause 3s]

[clear] Now you're going to sort each worry with one single question. Is this a real problem, happening now? Or a hypothetical problem, one that might happen someday?
A real problem is something like, my car is making a weird noise. I haven't answered that email. I have an unresolved argument with my sister. It's here, today.
A hypothetical problem often starts with what if. What if I lose my job. What if it's a serious illness. What if my kids don't turn out okay. It isn't happening. It might, perhaps, someday.
[pause 2s]
Go back through your list from the start, and for each worry, mark an R for real, or an H for hypothetical. If you're not writing, sort them in your mind, to the left or to the right.
A quick test can help. Can I do something about this in the next few days? If so, it's probably a real worry. If the answer is no, I can only wait or imagine, it's probably a hypothetical one.
[pause 4s]
[calm] Take your time. Some worries are a mix. What if my presentation goes badly, is hypothetical. But I haven't prepared my presentation yet, is real. You can separate them.
[reassuring] This sorting isn't a test. If you're unsure, put a question mark and move on. What matters is seeing more clearly, not being right.
[pause 3s]

[clear] Let's start with the real worries. For each one, look for a concrete next step. Not the whole solution, just the next step.
The car noise. Call the garage tomorrow morning. The waiting email. Reply in two lines at ten o'clock. The argument. Send a message suggesting you talk this weekend.
Make each step very precise. What, and when. If you're writing, note it next to the worry.
[pause 2s]
If the problem is huge, like a move or a conflict at work, break it down. The next step isn't resolve the conflict. It's write three sentences to prepare for the conversation. Small, doable, scheduled.
And if a real problem has no immediate solution, the step can be asking for help, or setting a time to think it over later.
[warm] Notice what it's like to turn a vague worry into a small, precise action. Often, the body relaxes a little as soon as it knows what it's going to do.
[pause 4s]

[calm, gentle] Now for the hypothetical worries. You can't solve these today, because they don't exist yet. Going over and over them doesn't stop them from happening. It just makes you carry their weight in advance.
And often, what we dread doesn't happen the way we imagined. Think of something you were worried about a year ago. Did it happen exactly that way?
[pause 3s]
And if something hard did happen, you faced it with the resources you had at the time.
[soft] Take them one by one. Simply acknowledge each one. This is a worry about the future. It's here because this matters to me.
Then let it be. If you're writing, you can draw a light line through it, or fold the page over on that side. If you're doing this in your mind, imagine placing it on a shelf.
You're not denying it. You're choosing not to deal with it now, because there's nothing to do with it, here, today.
[pause 4s]
[calm] If some hypothetical worries resist, ask yourself, is there one small, real thing I can do to prepare, or to take care of myself? If so, move it to the real side. If not, leave it on the shelf.
Over time, you may notice that some hypothetical worries come back often. They're old regulars. You can greet them, without throwing the door wide open.
[pause 2s]

[clear, grounded] The appointment is coming to an end. Let's close it, for real.
If you've been writing, fold your sheet in half, or close the note, and put it away in a drawer, a bag, somewhere separate. If you did it all in your head, imagine closing a notebook and setting it down on a table, far from you.
[pause 2s]
Tell yourself silently, firmly and kindly... The appointment is over. See you tomorrow, same time.
[pause 2s]
[gentle] Notice the effect of that sentence. Maybe a small relief. Maybe a part of you that doubts. What if I forget something? Nothing will be forgotten. It's all on the list, and the list is waiting for you.
[pause 3s]

[warm, clear] Now here's the most important instruction, the one that makes this technique work.
During the day, a worry will almost certainly come knocking again. That's normal. They're used to walking in unannounced.
When that happens, don't argue with it. Notice it, and simply tell it, I see you. We'll talk about it at the next appointment. You can even jot it down quickly on tomorrow's list.
Then go back to what you were doing. In the first few days, this may take a lot of reminders. Over time, many people find their worries wait their turn more patiently.
This isn't avoidance. You're not sweeping your worries under the rug. You're giving them a real appointment. That's exactly what lets your mind loosen its grip on them in between.
[pause 3s]

[calm, grounded] To finish, come back to the present through your body. Place both feet flat and press them gently into the floor. Feel how solid it is.
Rest your hands on your thighs. Notice their weight, their warmth.
[pause 2s]
Gently roll your shoulders back, once, twice. Slowly turn your head to one side, then the other. Your body also needs to know the appointment is over.
[pause 3s]
[soft] Let your breath settle. One breath in...
[pause 4s]
[slow] and one long breath out, releasing your shoulders.
[pause 6s]
[clear] Look around you, or open your eyes if they were closed. Choose three things you can see right now, and name them silently.
[pause 4s]
Listen for one sound, near or far.
[pause 4s]
[warm] Your worries are put away, and your next step is clear. The rest of the day belongs to you. And tomorrow, at the same time, your appointment will be waiting.
[warm, slowing down] Thank you for giving yourself this time.
