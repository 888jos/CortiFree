[calm, clear] Have you ever been through this? A hard week comes to an end. The project is handed in, the argument is settled, the exam is over. The problem is gone... and yet the tension is still there. In your shoulders, your stomach, your sleep.
[pause 1.5s]
Two American authors, Emily and Amelia Nagoski, offer a simple way to understand this gap. They separate the stressor from the stress.
The stressor is the cause. Work, money, a conflict, an endless line at the store.
The stress is the reaction in your body. Your heart speeding up, your muscles tensing, hormones circulating.
And here's the key idea. Dealing with the stressor doesn't necessarily deal with the stress. You can solve the problem, and still carry the reaction in your body.
[pause 1.5s]
Think of a gazelle chased by a lion. If it escapes, it's because it ran with all its strength. Its body used up the energy that stress mobilized, then returned to calm. The cycle is complete.
We, on the other hand, face stressors we can neither run away from nor fight. We stay seated in front of a screen, heart pounding, jaw clenched. That mobilized energy has nowhere to go.
It's a bit like starting the car, revving the engine, and then sitting in neutral all day long.
And if these cycles stay open day after day, the tension builds up, a bit like a pile of dishes that never gets washed.
A sink full of dishes isn't a disaster on day one. After a few weeks, it becomes a real weight.
[pause 1.5s]
This is what these authors call an unfinished stress cycle. It's more of an image than a scientific theory in the strict sense. But it fits with well established findings. Physical activity, rest, slow breathing and social connection help the body return to calm.
[pause 1.5s]
[warm] So what tells your body the danger has passed?
Moving, first. It's one of the most effective ways. Walking, dancing, running, anything that gets your body working.
Breathing slowly, with long out breaths.
Laughing, really laughing, with someone. Not a polite little laugh. A real fit of giggles, the kind that shakes your belly.
Connection and affection. A warm conversation, a hug that lasts a little while, stroking a pet.
Creating, too. Drawing, singing, writing down what you feel, playing music. Anything that lets what's inside find a way out.
And sometimes, crying. Crying isn't failure. For many people, it's a way of releasing pressure.
